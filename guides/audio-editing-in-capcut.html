Audio Editing in CapCut

Viewers forgive shaky video, but they never forgive bad audio. Music, voiceovers, fades and sound effects are half of a good edit — here is how to handle them in CapCut.

Adding music: the right track, the right volume

Tap "Audio" in the toolbar, then "Sounds" to browse CapCut's music library, or "Extracted" to take the audio from another video. Pick a track that matches the energy of your video — not just a popular one. Once added, the music appears as a track on the timeline: drag it to start where you want, trim it to length, and set its volume. The most important number in audio editing is the music level: for a voiceover video, keep music at 10–20 percent so the voice stays clear; for a montage with no talking, music can run at 70–100 percent. If you have to choose between music that is slightly too loud and slightly too quiet, choose quiet — viewers turn up quiet music, but they mute loud music.

Recording a voiceover

Tap "Audio", then "Voiceover", and hold the record button while you speak. Record in a quiet room, close to the phone, with the phone about a hand's width from your mouth — and record each section separately rather than one long take, so mistakes are easy to re-do. After recording, listen back with headphones: cut out breaths, pauses and mistakes with the split tool, and use the "noise reduction" option if the room had a hum. A clean voiceover recorded on a phone in a quiet room sounds better than a sloppy recording on an expensive microphone.

Fades: the professional touch

Music that starts and stops abruptly sounds amateur. Add a fade-in at the start of your music (about half a second) and a fade-out at the end (one second), using the fade controls on the audio clip. The same applies to voiceovers at the start and end of the video. Fades are small, but they are one of the clearest signs of a careful edit — viewers feel the difference even if they cannot name it. If your video ends on a music beat, cut the music exactly on the beat instead of fading; a beat cut is a stronger ending than a fade.

Ducking: keeping voice and music apart

Ducking means lowering the music automatically whenever someone speaks, and raising it again in the gaps. In CapCut, you can do this by hand: split the music track at each point where speech starts and ends, and lower the volume of the music segments under the speech. It takes a minute per video and makes a huge difference — the voice stays intelligible and the music still fills the gaps. Some versions offer an automatic ducking option; if yours does, try it first, then adjust the result by hand where it gets the levels wrong.

Sound effects: small, purposeful, rare

CapCut's sound effects library has whooshes, pops, clicks, camera shutters and hundreds more. Use them like salt: a whoosh on a fast transition, a pop when text appears, a shutter click on a photo change. One effect every few seconds is plenty; an effect on every cut is exhausting. Match the effect to the action — a whoosh for movement, a pop for appearance, silence for a dramatic pause. And keep effects quieter than the voice: if the viewer notices the effect before the content, it is too loud.

Fixing common audio problems

If your video's original audio is noisy, select the clip and try noise reduction, then lower the clip volume and let the music or voiceover carry the video. If the audio is out of sync with the lips, the clip's frame rate may not match the project — re-import it or nudge the audio track a few frames. If the exported video's audio sounds worse than in the editor, export at high quality: low-bitrate export hurts audio first, and the fix is the same as for video — raise the export quality. Always check the final export with headphones before posting.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use any song in CapCut?

CapCut's library is licensed for use in the app, but platform rules still apply — some tracks are restricted on business accounts or in certain countries.

How do I remove background noise?

Select the audio or video clip and use the noise reduction option. For heavy noise, record again in a quieter place — no tool fully fixes bad audio.

Why is my music too loud over my voice?

Lower the music track to 10–20 percent while the voice plays, and use fades and ducking to keep the two apart.
